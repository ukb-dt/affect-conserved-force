This means there are at least *two* separable systems that can destabilize:

**The affective/energetic system**: Monoamine dynamics, sleep-wake regulation, hedonic tone, motor activation. This creates the hypomania → mania spectrum of arousal and mood elevation.

**The reality-monitoring system**: Whatever circuitry adjudicates between "this thought is generated internally" vs "this is information about the external world." Salience attribution. The Bayesian priors that normally constrain interpretation. The system that says "this pattern you're seeing is probably coincidence" rather than "this pattern reveals hidden meaning."

In hypomania, only the first system is dysregulated. In full mania, *both* are.

The question is whether they're independently bistable or whether one destabilizes the other through coupling.

**Hypothesis 1**: They're coupled. Extreme dopaminergic hyperactivity (part of the manic state) directly disrupts salience processing. When everything feels intensely meaningful, the threshold for "this is real/important" drops. Coincidences become synchronicities. Internal thoughts acquire the phenomenological quality of external truths. The delusions are a *direct consequence* of the neurochemical state that also produces the affective symptoms.

This is somewhat supported by: dopamine agonists (like in Parkinson's treatment) can produce psychosis even without mood elevation. Amphetamine psychosis looks a lot like paranoid schizophrenia. There's overlap in the circuits.

**Hypothesis 2**: They're separately bistable but have a shared destabilizing factor. Sleep deprivation, for instance, destabilizes *both* mood regulation and reality testing. So does extreme stress. The systems can flip independently, but they tend to flip together because they share common regulatory inputs.

This would explain: why sleep deprivation can trigger both mania and psychosis. Why lithium (which affects multiple signaling cascades) stabilizes both. Why some people have "pure" depressive episodes with psychotic features—the reality-monitoring system flipped without the affective system going manic.

**Hypothesis 3**: They're independent, but in some individuals, they're *proximity-coupled*. The basins are close enough in state space that when the affective system jumps to the manic attractor, it perturbs the reality-monitoring system enough to kick it over its own threshold. But in other individuals (those who only experience hypomania), the basins are farther apart—you can be manic without destabilizing reality testing.

This would explain: the existence of bipolar II (hypomania without psychosis), the variability in psychotic features even among people with bipolar I, the fact that some people have their first psychotic break *during* a manic episode while others develop psychosis independently.

Is reality-testing just a threshold effect of dopamine concentration? (Hypothesis 1: one basin, parametrically dependent on dopamine)

Or is it a separate dynamical system with its own basins, but sharing regulatory inputs? (Hypothesis 2: coupled through common forcing)

Or is it fundamentally independent but with person-specific coupling strength? (Hypothesis 3: nearby basins with variable interaction terms)

Psychiatry doesn't distinguish these because it can't measure the state variables directly. It only sees the output: "patient is manic and delusional" vs "patient is hypomanic without delusions." The field treats this pragmatically (add an antipsychotic if there are delusions) but without mechanistic understanding of *why* some vessels require both stabilizers and why others don't.
